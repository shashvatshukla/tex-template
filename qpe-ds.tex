\title{Explaining quantum phase estimation using data structures}
\date{\today}
%\author{Shashvat Shukla}
\begin{abstract}
	The quantum phase estimation algorithm is a fundamental quantum computing technique used in physical simulation and linear algebraic tasks. 
	It is typically taught by stating the quantum circuit for quantum phase estimation and then proving its correctness by algebraic symbol manipulation. This approach rigorously establishes the correctness of the algorithm, but it does not provide a high-level algorithmic perspective on what the algorithm is doing.
	This paper explains the phase estimation algorithm using high-level concepts, by showing that quantum phase estimation employs elementary polynomial data structures from classical computer science. 
	Once the polynomial data structure concepts have been taught to students, the explanation of the quantum phase estimation algorithm can be stated in just a few lines.
	As quantum computing continues to grow in popularity and is taught in more diverse contexts, we hope this alternative approach to explaining phase estimation will be useful to students and instructors.
\end{abstract}
\maketitle



\section{Introduction}
	
% quantum computing, quantum algorithms, quantum phase estimation
Quantum computers can run new kinds of algorithms, and solve problems that are intractable on classical computers \cite{nielsen2010quantum}. Their power comes from quantum bits (qubits) which extend classical bits with features like superposition, interference, and entanglement, allowing them to store and process information more efficiently. Among the most important algorithms for quantum computers is the quantum phase estimation algorithm \cite{kitaev1995quantum}. It is a subroutine in important algorithms such as in the simulation of physical systems \cite{abrams1999quantum}, and in Shor’s factoring algorithm \cite{shor1994algorithms}. Quantum phase estimation is still routinely employed in novel quantum algorithms \cite{schmidhuber2025quartic, berry2024analyzing}.

% the problem we solve
Existing presentations of quantum phase estimation state the quantum circuit that implements the algorithm and present an analysis of the correctness of the circuit by means of algebraic symbol manipulation \cite{nielsen2010quantum, wong2022introduction}. These presentations compute the final state of the circuit by starting with the initial state and applying the unitary operations in the circuit in sequence, to derive what the final state of the circuit will be. While this approach is rigorous, we find the symbolic reasoning to be cumbersome. Moreover, we think that it obscures the algorithmic ideas of quantum phase estimation, since students are not explained how they may have discovered the algorithm by themselves, or how it may be similar to other algorithms in classical or quantum computer science. This could make it hard for students to remember how quantum phase estimation works, or to intuitively reason about variants of the algorithm without performing laborious derivations. It would be better if the rigorous algebraic details were presented only after some high-level ideas that capture how the algorithm works.

% how we solve it. accessibility, interestingness and connection to other fields. 
The contribution of this paper is to provide such an alternative explanation for quantum phase estimation, that is stated in terms of algorithmic ideas that are well-known in classical computer science. We show how data structures for polynomials and a divide-and-conquer framing explain the quantum phase estimation algorithm in a simple way. Note that data structures are an essential concept in the study of classical algorithms. A common strategy for designing a new efficient algorithm is to invent a clever data structure that makes the storage and processing of information required for that computational task more efficient. Although quantum phase estimation has not been stated in this way before, we show how such a retrospective data structure approach simplifies its presentation and points to the source of the quantum advantage. Similarly, divide-and-conquer is a key algorithmic approach in computer science for designing efficient algorithms. In quantum phase estimation, divide-and-conquer appears within the subroutine known as the quantum Fourier transform (QFT). While not all unitary transformations have efficient circuits, it is possible to efficiently perform QFT because the circuit for it can be constructed using a divide-and-conquer approach on the unitary matrix for QFT. 

% why it has broad appeal
An ever-larger community of scientists and engineers will need at least a working understanding of quantum phase estimation. One reason for this is that quantum computing is a growing scientific area and industry in its own right within which students may build their careers. Also, it is expected that quantum computers running quantum phase estimation will be applied in various branches of science, and students who use the technique may need just a conceptual understanding of how it works. The presentation in this paper is suitable for both undergraduate and graduate students, and requires just an introductory course to linear algebra as a prerequisite. It is particularly useful for students with electrical engineering and computer science backgrounds for whom these concepts will be more familiar. 
However, we think it is of general interest, and even students who have a physics background or are studying quantum computing in depth can be presented with our conceptual explanation before learning the usual algebraic details. 

With the rapid growth of university courses and programs that cover quantum computing, many instructors across physics and other departments will find themselves teaching or applying it. We hope our conceptual explanation will be helpful to these instructors.

We first review classical data structures for representing polynomials, then show how these motivate the Discrete and Fast Fourier transforms, and finally explain the quantum analogues of these ideas. 	\subsection{Polynomial data structures}	
	
	% Coefficient representation
	The material in this subsection can be motivated by asking students to consider how polynomials might be represented in a classical computer. 
	The straightforward approach, the \textbf{coefficient representation} of a polynomial \cite{cormen2009algorithms_ch30} involves storing a vector (or list) of all its coefficients. For example, the polynomial $p(x) = a_{N-1}x^{N-1} + ... + a_0$ is stored as the vector $a = [a_0 ... a_{N-1}]$. 
	
	% Point value representation
	There are other data structures for polynomials. Consider the \textbf{point-value representation} of a polynomial \cite{cormen2009algorithms_ch30}, which uses the fact that a polynomial, $p$, with degree less than $N$ is uniquely determined by a set of $N$ distinct points $\{(x_0,p(x_0)), ..., (x_{N-1},p(x_{N-1}))\}$. For example, storing two points represents the unique line that passes through them. For a polynomial of degree less than $N$, we can evaluate it at $N$ different points and store those points. Note that we are free to choose any set of $N$ distinct input values for this purpose, and regardless of our choice these points uniquely identify the polynomial. It is convenient to use the same set of inputs for all the different polynomials we are storing. If we use the inputs $\{x_0, ..., x_{N-1}\}$ to create point-value representations, then any polynomial, $p$, of degree up to $N$ can be stored as the vector $[p(x_0)...p(x_{N-1})]$. This is explained with examples in Figure \ref{fig:polyinterpolation}.
	
	
\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{poly_interpolation}
	\caption{There is only one polynomial of degree less than four that passes through the four points $\{(0,1),(1,1),(2,1),(3,7)\}$, and it is $x^3 -3x^2+2x+1$. Similarly the polynomial $-x^2+4x+4$ is the only polynomial of degree less than four that passes through the four points $\{(0,4),(1,7),(2,8),(3,7)\}$. Thus we may represent the polynomials by these sets of points. If we use inputs $x=0,1,2,3$ to create point-value representations, then we may simply store $p_1$ as $[1,1,1,7]$, and $p_2$ as $[4,7,8,7]$.}
	\label{fig:polyinterpolation}
\end{figure}
	
	% The coefficient representation makes multiplication easier, dont need to bring in Horner's rule at all
	The point-value representation is significant in classical algorithms because multiplying two polynomials that are represented in this way is efficient. For two polynomials $p$ and $q$, their product $r = p \cdot q$ can be obtained simply by multiplying their stored values coordinate-wise:
	\begin{equation}
	[r(x_0), \ldots, r(x_{N-1})] = [p(x_0)q(x_0), \ldots, p(x_{N-1})q(x_{N-1})].
	\end{equation}
	This procedure works as long as the polynomials $p$ and $q$ each have degree less than $N/2$ so that the resulting product $r$ still has degree less than $N$ (and can thus be uniquely determined by $N$ points). Multiplication of polynomials in the point-value representation only requires $\mathcal{O}(N)$ multiplications of numbers. In contrast, multiplication of polynomials in the coefficient representation is done by long multiplication \cite{west2011introduction}, which requires $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ additions and multiplications of numbers to combine and sum cross-terms.
	
% Switching data structure representations to benefit from the faster multiplication operation
The coefficient representation is the more common and natural choice, so one would hope to store polynomials in the coefficient representation but use a method to convert between the two representations in order to take advantage of the faster multiplication algorithm in the point-value representation. However this idea only works if we convert between representations efficiently. The naive approach to converting from coefficients to point values requires multiplying by a Vandermonde matrix \cite{klinger1967vandermonde}. For example, given $p(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_{N-1}x^{N-1}$ and fixed evaluation points $\{x_0, \ldots, x_{N-1}\}$, the point value representation is obtained by:
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
	p(x_0) \\
	p(x_1) \\
	\vdots \\
	p(x_{N-1})
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
	1 & x_0 & x_0^2 & \cdots & x_0^{N-1} \\
	1 & x_1 & x_1^2 & \cdots & x_1^{N-1} \\
	\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
	1 & x_{N-1} & x_{N-1}^2 & \cdots & x_{N-1}^{N-1}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
	a_0 \\ a_1 \\ \vdots \\ a_{N-1}
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

In general, multiplying a matrix onto a vector requires $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ elementary operations, and thus the naive approach to convert between the coefficient and point-value representations requires $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ time. However directly performing the multiplication in the coefficient representation also takes $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ time, and thus a slow $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ conversion can offer us no shortcuts to multiplying polynomials. 

% DFT and FFT
However it is possible to convert representations in $\mathcal{O}(N\log N)$ time if we choose a special set of evaluation points to construct the point-value representation. Specifically, if we choose the $N$-th roots of unity (i.e. powers of $\omega_N = e^{i\frac{2\pi}{N}}$) then we get the following Vandermonde matrix:
\begin{equation}
F_N =
\begin{bmatrix}
	1 & 1 & 1 & \cdots & 1 \\
	1 & \omega_N & \omega_N^2 & \cdots & \omega_N^{N-1} \\
	1 & \omega_N^2 & \omega_N^{2\cdot 2} & \cdots & \omega_N^{2(N-1)} \\
	\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
	1 & \omega_N^{N-1} & \omega_N^{2(N-1)} & \cdots & \omega_N^{(N-1)(N-1)}
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
This matrix ($F_N$) is called the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) matrix, and it is important in signal processing, functional analysis and abstract algebra. Applying it to the coefficient vector $[a_0, \ldots, a_{N-1}]$ computes the values $p(1), p(\omega_N), \ldots, p(\omega_N^{N-1})$. The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is a divide-and-conquer method to multiply $F_N$ onto a vector in only $\mathcal{O}(N\log N)$ time, making the conversion between coefficient and point-value representations more efficient. 

The key idea is to split the $N$ coefficients $(a_0, \ldots, a_{N-1})$ into their even- and odd-indexed parts, forming two smaller polynomials of size $N/2$. These smaller polynomials are only evaluated at even powers of $\omega_N$ and so only need to be evaluated at half as many inputs. Define $p_{\text{even}}(x) = a_0 + a_2 x + a_4 x^2 + ... $ and $p_{\text{odd}}(x) = a_1 + a_3 x + a_5 x^2 + ... $, so that $p(x) \equiv  p_{\text{even}}(x^2) + x \cdot p_{\text{odd}}(x^2)$.


Notice that:
	\begin{equation}
	\omega_N^{2(i + N/2)} = \omega_N^{2i}\,\omega_N^{N} = \omega_N^{2i},
	\end{equation}
	which means,
	\begin{equation}
	p_{\text{even}}((\omega_N^{i + N/2})^2) = p_{\text{even}}((\omega_N^i)^2), \text{and}
	\end{equation}
	\begin{equation}
	p_{\text{odd}}((\omega_N^{i + N/2})^2) = p_{\text{odd}}((\omega_N^i)^2).
	\end{equation}
	Thus we only need to evaluate $p_{\text{even}}(x^2)$ (or $p_{\text{odd}}(x^2)$) at half the inputs $\omega_N^i$, namely when $i = 0, 1, ..., N/2 -1$, as the remaining values will be repetitions. We can then combine the evaluations using simple additions and multiplications as:
	\begin{equation}
		p(\omega_N^k) = p_{\text{even}}(\omega_N^{2k}) + \omega_N^k\, p_{\text{odd}}(\omega_N^{2k}), \qquad k = 0, \ldots, N - 1.
	\end{equation}

	The time complexity of this approach is thus given by the recursive function $T(N) = 2\cdot T(N/2) + \mathcal{O}(N)$, as each problem of evaluating the polynomial is divided into two polynomial evaluation problems of half the size, and a linear time combination step. Solving this recursion \cite{bentley1980general} shows that the Fast Fourier Transform reduces the time complexity of converting polynomial representations from $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(N\log N)$.
		
	
	\subsection{Quantum data structures}
	
	% How to motivate this material
	The material in this subsection can be motivated as the quantum analogues of the classical polynomial data structures from the previous subsection.
	
	% Amplitude encoding, ~ of polynomial data structures
	Quantum computers are capable of performing some computations on large vectors efficiently by encoding the data into the amplitudes of a quantum state. Since an $n$ qubit quantum state is described using a vector of $2^n$ amplitudes, computations on vectors encoded as quantum states are capable of achieving large advantages in space and time complexity. The standard encoding, known as the \textbf{amplitude encoding} of a vector, is the quantum state obtained by normalising the vector and putting the coefficients into amplitudes of a quantum state, for example as: $v= [v_0 ... v_{N-1}] \rightarrow \frac{1}{\norm{v}} \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} v_i \ket{i}$. Our explanation of QPE features the amplitude encodings of the coefficient and point-value representations of polynomials.
	
	% DFT , QFT
	The primary way that quantum computers manipulate amplitude encoded vectors is by unitary operations. 
	When normalised by a $\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}$ factor, the Discrete Fourier Transform is a unitary matrix. Just as the Discrete Fourier Transform converts the coefficient representation to the point-value representation, its quantum circuit version converts the \textit{amplitude encoding} of the coefficient representation to the \textit{amplitude encoding} of the point-value representation (see Table \ref{tbl:data_structures}). This circuit, and the transformation it implements, are both called the Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT). The circuit can be implemented efficiently by using a divide-and-conquer approach just like the Fast Fourier Transform \cite{paradisi2004presentation}. The action of QFT on an $N$-dimensional computational basis state can be decomposed into some simple operations and a smaller instance of QFT on an $N/2$-dimensional computational basis state as:
	
	\begin{align}
		QFT_N \ket{j}_{\text{n qubits}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ \omega_N^j \\ \vdots \\ \omega_N^{j(N-1)}\end{pmatrix} &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{N/2}}\begin{psmallmatrix}1 \\ \omega_N^{2j} \\ \vdots \\ \omega_N^{j(N-2)}\end{psmallmatrix} \otimes \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ \omega_N^j\end{pmatrix} \nonumber \\
		&= QFT_{N/2} \ket{j \mod (N/2)}_{\text{n-1 qubits}} \otimes \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ \omega_N^j\end{pmatrix},
	\end{align}
where $n= \log_2 N$. This decomposition can be repeatedly applied until the smallest QFT circuit is reached, which is $QFT_2$ and it is equal to a single Hadamard gate. The circuit to prepare a qubit in the state $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{psmallmatrix}1 \\ \omega_N^j\end{psmallmatrix}$ starting from $\ket{j}$ is shown in Figure \ref{fig:qft-one-step}. It relies on the base-2 representation of $\ket{j}$ as $\ket{b_{n-1}\dots b_0}$, and the product $\omega_N^j = \omega_{2^1}^{b_{n-1}}\dots\omega_{2^{n-1}}^{b_1}\omega_{2^n}^{b_0}.$

	Figure \ref{fig:qft-recursion} shows how this divide-and-conquer approach gives a recursive definition of the QFT circuit. When this circuit equality is repeatedly applied to decompose the circuit for $QFT_N$ into gates, first a sequence of ``P'' gates are obtained. These ``P'' gates can be decomposed further into one- and two- qubit gates by replacing them with the circuit shown in Figure \ref{fig:qft-one-step}. Figure \ref{fig:qft-circuit} shows an explicit definition of the QFT circuit in terms of elementary logic gates once all these decomposition steps are performed. 
	
	The QPE algorithm uses the inverse-QFT circuit. This can be obtained by simply reversing the QFT circuit and inverting every gate.

	\section{Quantum Phase Estimation}
	\label{sec:qpe}
	% Problem and set up
	
	\subsection{Problem}
	
	This subsection motivates the Quantum Phase Estimation problem as a natural quantum analogue of a classical eigenvalue problem. Since quantum computers natively operate large matrices and vectors, the use of quantum computers for linear algebraic tasks has been an active field of research.
	
	Classical algorithms take $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ time to compute \cite{trefethen2022numerical} all the eigenvalues of a given matrix. On the other hand, if an eigenvector $v$ of a matrix $A$ is known, the \textit{corresponding eigenvalue} can be found in $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ time by computing $\frac{v^T A v}{v^T v}$.  
	
	Quantum phase estimation is the quantum analogue of the task of computing the \textit{corresponding eigenvalue} when given a matrix and an eigenvector. A key difference between the QPE problem and its classical counterpart is how the data is provided. In QPE, the eigenvector is given as a quantum state in which the vector is amplitude encoded, and the matrix is given as a quantum circuit of which we are given a description. Note that this complicates the comparison of the classical and quantum algorithms, as they solve analogous problems but not exactly the same problem. One way to put them on an equal footing is to view QPE as an extra tool for the corresponding eigenvalue problem that can be used if the input data meets the extra requirements of the quantum algorithm. This can occur when the matrix and eigenvector are too large to operate on directly but still have some structure that makes it easy to work with them on a quantum computer. Notably, this occurs in the case of Hamiltonian simulation, where the unitary operations corresponding to the time evolutions of many interesting Hamiltonians can be implemented as efficient quantum circuits\cite{aspuru2005simulated}.
	
	 We may state the QPE problem formally as follows: 
	
	\begin{problem*}
		Let $U$ denote a unitary matrix, and let $\ket{\psi} \in \mathbb{C}^N$ be an eigenvector of $U$ with corresponding eigenvalue $\omega_N^k = e^{i 2 \pi k/N}$ for some $k\in \{0,1,...,N-1\}$, the objective is to determine $k$ given:
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item $\ket{\psi}$ as one copy of the quantum state, and
			\item access to $U$ via black-box calls to a qudit-controlled-$U^d$ gate.
		\end{enumerate} 
	\end{problem*}

	We define the qudit-controlled-$U^d$ gate, as the gate that acts in the following manner:
	$(C_dU^d) \ket{j}\ket{\phi} = \ket{j}U^j\ket{\phi}$. This is easily motivated as a generalisation of the standard controlled-$U$ gate, which either applies the $U$ gate 0 or 1 times, depending on the value of the controlling qubit. Qudits are \textit{d}-level quantum systems that generalise qubits, and have a computational basis $\{\ket{0},\ket{1},...,\ket{d-1}\}$. The qudit-controlled-$U^d$ gate applies the $U$ gate $j$ times, if the value of the controlling qudit is $j$. This allows us to apply $U$ different numbers of times in a controlled way. 
	
	Note that all unitaries have eigenvalues of the form $e^i\theta$, since unitaries are norm-preserving linear maps. Here we have focussed on the case where the eigenvalue to be determined has an angle that is precisely one of the $N$-th roots of unity. This greatly simplifies the analysis of the algorithm while still preserving its conceptual core. 
	
	\subsection{Algorithm}
	
	
	This subsection shows how the high-level concepts above can be used to explain the QPE algorithm in just a few lines. 
	 
	 
	\begin{figure}
		\centering
		\title{Quantum Phase Estimation Algorithm}
		
		\includegraphics{figure-generation/qpe-circuit/qpe-circuit.pdf}
		
		\caption{High level circuit explaining the Quantum Phase Estimation Algorithm. The algorithm has two steps, separated by the dashed line. Step 1 prepares $\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}  (\omega^{k}_N)^j \ket{j} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}  (\omega^{j}_N)^k \ket{j}$, which is the amplitude encoding of the point-value representation of the polynomial $x^k$. Step 2 converts to the amplitude encoding of the coefficient representation, yielding $k$.}
		\label{fig:qpe-circuit}
	\end{figure}

	The Quantum Phase Estimation algorithm is a quantum circuit (Figure \ref{fig:qpe-circuit}) that uses the qudit-controlled-$U^d$ gate and the quantum state $\ket{\psi}$ to determine the eigenvalue of $\ket{\psi}$ with respect to $U$. 
	This circuit has two parts. The first part \textbf{prepares an amplitude encoding of the point-value representation of the polynomial} $\bf{x^k}$. This can be shown as follows:
	\begin{eqnarray}
		&C_dU^d [\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1} \ket{j}\ket{\psi}] \nonumber \\
		&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}  (\omega^{k}_N)^j \ket{j} \ket{\psi}  &(\text{Since } U\ket{\psi} = \omega_N^k \ket{\psi}) \nonumber \\
		&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}  (\omega^{j}_N)^k \ket{j} \ket{\psi}.
	\end{eqnarray}
	Notice that the resultant state has amplitudes equal to taking each root of unity to the $k$-th power, and is thus an encoding of the polynomial $x^k$. 
	
	The second part \textbf{converts to the amplitude encoding of the coefficient representation of the polynomial} using the inverse-QFT, giving us the outcome $\ket{k}$, since the encoded polynomial was $x^k$. 
	
	The running time of this algorithm depends heavily on the circuit complexity of implementing the qudit-controlled-$U^d$ operation. When this can be done efficiently in time that is polylogarithmic in $N$, the QPE algorithm is capable of achieving exponential speedups over classical algorithms that use time polynomial in $N$ to operate on the explicit matrix, $U$.
	
	When the eigenvalue is not precisely an $N$-th root of unity, two strategies apply. First, a higher-dimensional qudit register can be used to approximate the eigenvalue more closely. It might turn out for a larger choice of $N$, the eigenvalue is exactly a root of unity. To increase $N$ however requires a larger qudit register, more applications of $U$, and a larger inverse-QFT circuit. Second, even when the eigenvalue is not exactly a root of unity, the algorithm outputs the nearest root of unity with high probability, since the fidelity between the true state prepared and the ideal state (i.e. amplitude encoding of point-value representation of $x^k$) is large. The success probability increases as the eigenvalue approaches a root of unity. Thus increasing $N$ improves both the precision of the approximation and the probability of success, at the cost of a larger quantum circuit. The detailed analysis of this can be found in standard algebraic presentations \cite{nielsen2010quantum}. 
	
	\section{Conclusion}
	
	This paper first explained the following high-level computer science concepts: data structures for polynomials, the discrete and fast Fourier transforms, amplitude encodings and the quantum Fourier transform. The two main themes in these ideas were: 1) the study of data structures, which concerns different ways in which data can be stored and their relative strengths and weaknesses; and 2) divide-and-conquer approaches to algorithm design.
	
	Our paper is then able to explain the quantum phase estimation algorithm very quickly, as the high level concepts make it intuitive to understand what the algorithm is doing. We also showed how this intuitive understanding of what the algorithm is doing makes it easy to derive the quantum circuit to be used. Using our approach saves instructors and students from a complex proof relying on algebraic symbol manipulation that is used in most presentations of QPE. In order to receive these benefits however our explanation relies on teaching some material on data structures which is not typical of quantum computing courses. We believe this material on data structures is easy to grasp as it mostly serves to give names and interpretations to mathematical concepts. These concepts then help the student understand and remember the mathematical details of QPE.
	
	We believe this teaching approach leaves students with a number of easy-to-remember and reusable computer science ideas which they may combine to develop quantum algorithms themselves. In particular, data structures are emerging as a new perspective to describe quantum algorithms. A data structure perspective has similarly been used to explain the Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm \cite{firstimpression_dj} in an intuitive way. Data structure approaches have also been suggested for quantum algorithmic primitives \cite{kallaugher2025design,zhao2016fast} that can be used by programmers without the need for understanding underlying quantum information. We hope further investigations will recast more quantum algorithms in terms of data structures, and use the perspective to develop new algorithms.
